From a young age, Mom made sure we were raised to recognize the value of money, a lesson she demonstrated through her passion for coupons and free membership perks. 

I remember weekends spent watching [*Extreme Couponing*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IJR6U6O6sIc)[¹](#fn1) with satisfaction, amazed as shoppers paid only a few dollars for a massive haul. As I grew older, I noticed Mom had a knack for calculating the tiniest amounts she could save through discounts. She is always happy to be the designated payer (provided we pay her back) because she carries at least ten different credit cards, all with varying cashback and reward programs. She’s likely signed up for a dozen digital ones too, plus 30 other payment apps and membership logins. In her drawers, she stores vouchers from eons ago. Most are technically expired, but because paid vouchers legally can't have an expiration date, Mom manages to redeem them for goods, even if inflation has depreciated their value.

It is no surprise that Mom takes an extra ten minutes at the checkout counter figuring out which combination of coupons or memberships yields the highest discount. More often than not, it takes a senior store manager to understand the rules as clearly as she does. Even more challenging is getting modern scanners to accept her coupons that outlast the system's latest update. I’ve watched Mom settle a single bill using three different methods: a promo code, a gift card, and the tiniest remaining balance put on a credit card. Always a credit card, never cash[²](#fn2).

Naturally, this thrifty mindset trickled down to me. I never passed on a good deal. Back when my brother and I started taking the bus on our own in fourth grade, Mom had us track our allowances and leftover bus fare on a yellow legal pad. She made sure we always counted the coins in our little wallets (mine was a blue camouflage print that I bought at a garage sale) and taught us to log ***Miscellaneous*** when the balances didn’t match.

We would play treasure hunt, competing to find the most change by cracking open bus cushions and digging for coins covered in muck, chewed gum, and dead cockroaches. I remember hitting the jackpot once—NT$76 (one 50, one penny, two dimes, and a nickel) on a single hour-long bus ride to soccer practice. We split the earnings and saved it all.

I proudly logged any money I earned from extra chores (dishwashing and sou chef didn't count), stuffed cash from Gran, red envelopes, and school performances. Over the years, I maintained a long, manually written ledger of dates, descriptions, cash in, cash out, and my total balance.

I also rarely dipped into my little pool of savings. Even when I did, I’d spend directly from unlogged change just to avoid recording an expense that would show a decrease on my balance. Believe it or not, ***Allowance*** was one of the first long words I learned to spell which I associated it purely with hard-earned change. It wasn't until I took Accounting Principles years later that I learned it also means: **a reserve set aside in expectation of expenses that will be incurred at a future date.**
